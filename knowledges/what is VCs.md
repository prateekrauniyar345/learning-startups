# **Venture Capital (VC): How It Works, How VCs Make Money, and Real Examples**

**Venture capital (VC)** is a type of investment where investors provide money to young companies, usually startups, that have the potential to grow very quickly.

In exchange, investors receive **ownership (equity)** in the company.

Unlike banks, venture capitalists generally don't expect startups to repay the investment through monthly loan payments. Instead, they hope the company becomes much more valuable, allowing them to sell their ownership for a profit.

## **3. How do VC firms make money?**

VC firms generally earn money in two main ways.

**Management fees:** A fund might charge approximately 2% annually on committed capital during its investment period. For a $200 million fund, that would be $4 million annually, used to support operations, salaries, research, and other expenses. Actual fee structures vary.

**Carried interest (carry):** The VC managers commonly receive about 20% of investment profits, subject to the fund's agreement.

For a simplified illustration, suppose a $200 million fund eventually returns $600 million.

| Item                        | Amount |
| --------------------------- | ------ |
| Original investment capital | $200M  |
| Total proceeds              | $600M  |
| Investment profit           | $400M  |
| VC managers' 20% carry      | $80M   |
| Remaining profit for LPs    | $320M  |

This example ignores fees, expenses, preferred returns, and other fund-specific provisions.

## **4. The different startup funding stages**

1. **Pre-seed: Idea and early prototype**

   Founders are testing whether their idea solves a real problem. Funding often comes from savings, angel investors, accelerators, or early-stage funds.
2. **Seed: Building and finding customers**

   The company develops its product, attracts early users, and searches for product-market fit.
3. **Series A: Proving a repeatable business**

   The startup has meaningful traction and wants to grow its team, sales, and operations.
4. **Series B and C: Scaling**

   The company expands into new markets, develops additional products, and grows its customer base.
5. **Late stage: Preparing for a major exit**

   The business may raise substantial funding before an acquisition or public listing.

Funding amounts vary enormously by industry, geography, and market conditions. A biotech company may need substantially more capital than a software startup at the same stage.

## **6. What do venture capitalists actually do every day?**

VC work involves considerably more than giving money to startups.

A typical VC investor spends time finding promising founders, meeting startup teams, analyzing financial statements, evaluating markets and competitors, negotiating investment terms, and supporting existing portfolio companies.

For example, a VC evaluating an AI startup might ask:

- Does this product solve a problem customers will pay to fix?
- How large could the market become?
- Why is this startup better than its competitors?
- How much revenue is it generating, and how quickly is it growing?
- Can the founders build a company worth $1 billion or more?
- What could cause the investment to fail?

After investing, VCs may help recruit executives, introduce customers, advise on business strategy, and connect founders with future investors. Some also take seats on the company's board of directors.

## **7. Why do VCs invest in risky startups?**

Because venture capital follows a **power-law return pattern**: a small number of extremely successful investments can generate most of a fund's returns.

## **9. What happens when a startup becomes successful?**

VC investors usually realize their returns through an **exit**.

There are three common possibilities:

| Exit type      | What happens                                                              |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Acquisition    | Another company buys the startup, potentially paying cash or shares       |
| IPO            | The company lists its shares on a public stock exchange                   |
| Secondary sale | An investor sells shares to another investor before an IPO or acquisition |

For example, Meta's acquisition of Instagram in 2012 created an exit opportunity for Instagram's early investors. An IPO can also provide liquidity, although investors may be subject to restrictions before selling.

An important point is that a company being valued at $1 billion does not mean its investors have received $1 billion in cash. Until shares are sold, much of the value may remain unrealized.

## **11. How can someone become a venture capitalist?**

Given your earlier question about progressing from an internship to senior leadership at Google, VC is another possible career direction, but it has a different structure.

There are several ways to enter the industry. Some people begin in investment banking or consulting, others build startups, and others develop expertise in technology, product management, or corporate strategy.

A common investment career progression looks like this:

1. **Analyst (0–3 years):** Research markets, identify startups, and support investment analysis.
2. **Associate (2–5+ years):** Evaluate opportunities, conduct due diligence, and work with founders.
3. **Principal / Vice President (5–10+ years):** Lead investment opportunities and help manage portfolio companies.
4. **Partner / General Partner (10–15+ years):** Make major investment decisions, raise funds, and manage investor relationships.

These are rough experience ranges, not a guaranteed promotion ladder. Some successful founders become VC partners without following any of these steps.

## **12. Can a software engineer become a VC?**

Absolutely. A software engineer with strong AI, cloud computing, cybersecurity, or data infrastructure expertise can be valuable to a VC firm.

For instance, an AI-focused investor needs to understand whether a startup's technology is genuinely differentiated, whether it can scale, and whether competitors can easily reproduce it.

Someone who works as a software engineer at Google for several years, becomes a technical leader, and develops strong industry connections could eventually move into venture investing, especially in enterprise software or AI.

However, technical knowledge alone is insufficient. Investors also need financial analysis, market judgment, networking, negotiation, and the ability to identify exceptional founding teams.

The most interesting part of venture capital is that **VCs are not simply betting on technology. They are betting on founders, markets, execution, and the possibility of extraordinary growth.**
