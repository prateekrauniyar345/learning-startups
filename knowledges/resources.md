To understand startups and venture capital seriously, I recommend learning from three perspectives: **the founder, the investor, and the business operator**.

A founder needs to know how to identify problems and build products. An investor needs to understand valuations, funding rounds, equity, and risk. An operator needs to understand revenue, hiring, competition, cash flow, and why companies survive or fail.

I've selected **3 articles, 3 websites, and 3 books** that complement each other. Together, they provide a strong foundation in the startup ecosystem.

## **4. The order I recommend: A 12-week learning plan**

Don't read all nine resources randomly. Follow the order below so each topic builds on the previous one.

**Your learning progress**

0 of 6 stages

Weeks 1–2: Understand startup ideas

Read How to Get Startup Ideas and begin The Lean Startup.

Practical exercise: Identify 3 real problems and interview 5 potential customers.

Weeks 3–4: Learn how startups launch

Read Do Things That Don't Scale and explore Startup School.

Practical exercise: Design a simple MVP and explain who would pay for it.

Weeks 5–6: Understand fundraising

Read A Guide to Seed Fundraising and begin Venture Deals.

Practical exercise: Calculate a hypothetical valuation, funding round, and founder dilution.

Weeks 7–8: Understand venture economics

Explore Carta Learn and finish Venture Deals.

Practical exercise: Build a cap table with founders, investors, and employee equity.

Weeks 9–10: Learn how companies survive

Read The Hard Thing About Hard Things and First Round Review.

Practical exercise: Calculate burn rate, runway, gross margin, and customer acquisition cost.

Weeks 11–12: Think like a founder and investor

Review all resources and analyze a real startup.

Practical exercise: Create a pitch deck, financial model, and investment recommendation.

## **6. One practical project that will make the knowledge stick**

I would strongly recommend creating a fictional startup as you study.

For example, imagine building an AI education platform that helps university students learn mathematics.

You could practice developing its business plan, calculating how much money it needs, deciding whether to bootstrap or raise VC funding, estimating how much equity investors receive, and modeling what happens if the business succeeds or fails.

By the end, you should be able to answer questions such as:

- If my startup is worth $5 million and an investor offers $2 million, how much ownership should they receive?
- How can a company valued at $100 million still go bankrupt?
- Why would a VC invest in a company losing $10 million annually?
- How do investors decide whether to invest $500,000 or $20 million?
- Why might founders who started with 100% ownership end up with less than 20%?
- Why can a profitable startup be a poor VC investment?

**My strongest recommendation:** Start with *The Lean Startup*, study Y Combinator's free material alongside it, then read *Venture Deals*. Finish with *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*.

These nine resources won't make anyone unbeatable on their own, but combined with real startup case studies and financial modeling, they'll give you a much stronger understanding than simply memorizing venture capital terminology.
